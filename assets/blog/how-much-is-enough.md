## Working thesis

When I ask myself "How much is enough?", I do not mainly mean salary. I mean money as capital: the amount of wealth that would make my life less dependent on the next paycheck and more dependent on my own decisions.

The question is not only financial. It is also about time, work, family, responsibility, and what I actually want a normal week to look like.

## Intro: what the research seems to say

The empirical answer starts with a small problem: most research on money and happiness talks about income, not wealth. Income is the flow of money into life. Wealth is the stock of money and assets already built up. Salary can solve monthly problems, but wealth can change the amount of risk I am able to take and the amount of time I can decide over myself.

The famous older result from Kahneman and Deaton was that in the United States, emotional well-being rose with income up to around $75,000 of annual household income, while life evaluation continued to rise with higher income. Later, Jebb, Tay, Diener, and Oishi used Gallup World Poll data from more than 1.7 million people and estimated global "satiation" points around $95,000 for life evaluation and $60,000 to $75,000 for emotional well-being, with big differences between regions. These numbers are useful, but I should not treat them as my personal target. They are study-specific, US-dollar, context-dependent numbers.

The picture also became more nuanced. Killingsworth later found, using experience sampling, that experienced well-being continued to rise with income and did not clearly stop at $75,000. In 2023, Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers worked together on an adversarial collaboration and found something more careful: for the least happy people, income seems to reduce unhappiness only up to a point, while for happier people the relationship can continue upward. My current takeaway is: money can help, especially when it removes stress and gives options, but there is probably no magic income number where life suddenly becomes solved.

For wealth, the research is a bit closer to what this article is about. A German study using Panel on Household Finances data found that wealth matters in addition to income, especially financial assets and debt. Liquid assets seem important because they are usable as a buffer, while unsecured debt is associated with lower life satisfaction. A lottery study from Sweden is also interesting because it comes closer to a causal wealth effect: large lottery winners had higher overall life satisfaction even many years later, but the effects on day-to-day happiness and mental health were smaller.

This is where Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's idea of flow feels relevant to me. In *Flow*, he separates simple pleasure from deeper enjoyment. Flow is less about comfort and more about being fully involved in something that has challenge, skill, feedback, and attention. That makes the money question less "How much can I consume?" and more "How much money would help me arrange my days around work, people, and projects where flow is more likely?"

## The question I actually want to answer

Enough for what?

- Enough to pay normal living costs without stress.
- Enough to have a safety net when something goes wrong.
- Enough to choose projects more freely.
- Enough to try a different work setup, for example freelancing or building something myself.
- Enough to take responsibility for family, if that becomes relevant.
- Enough to contribute to things I care about without calculating every euro.

This is different from wanting to buy more expensive things. For me, being rich is less about being able to buy luxury and more about needing less approval, less external status, and less forced schedule.

## Salary, wealth, and time

Salary is useful because it solves recurring problems. It pays rent, food, travel, insurance, and hobbies. But salary also usually comes with dependence: a fixed schedule, an employer, limited upside, and sometimes a feeling that even a good job is still a job.

Wealth changes something else. It does not automatically make a day meaningful, but it can buy time, reduce pressure, and increase the number of reasonable choices.

This is probably the part I care about most: if I had enough capital, I could decide more freely which projects I work on, how much risk I take, and how my week is structured.

## What enough means to me right now

My current version of enough is not about increasing expenses. I do not have a big wish list of expensive things. The attractive part is independence.

Enough would mean:

- my basic lifestyle is covered by assets, not only by salary
- I can try work that is more self-directed
- I can choose projects for interest and usefulness, not only income
- I can slow down without feeling like I failed
- I can still keep building wealth, but without chasing an unclear finish line

The self-earned part also matters to me. Not because inherited money would be useless, but because building it myself would feel like a long project with effort, energy, and decisions inside it. It would feel different to harvest the result of that.

## A numbers section

This section should be practical, but not fake-precise.

Possible structure:

1. Start with current monthly living costs.
2. Convert that into annual expenses.
3. Compare it with a rough withdrawal-rate approach, for example the 4% rule.
4. Compare it with a dividend/cash-flow approach, for example 2.5% net dividends.
5. Add margin for taxes, inflation, healthcare, family, and location changes.
6. Separate "minimum independence" from "comfortable independence".

Example draft numbers to refine later:

- Living costs: around 2,000 EUR per month.
- Annual costs: around 24,000 EUR.
- Rough 4% rule target: around 600,000 EUR.
- Rough 2.5% net dividend target: around 960,000 EUR.

These are not final numbers. In a cheaper place they might work better, in an expensive city or with family they might not be enough. The more useful metric for me might be:

> average monthly dividend income / average monthly expenses

Right now this is not a 0 or 1 goal. It is a percentage that can slowly increase. If it is 10%, that already means part of my life is covered. If it is 50%, the pressure changes. If it is 100%, the question becomes what I actually want to do with the freedom.

## A values section without numbers

Before calculating the number, I should answer the question without numbers:

- Where do I want to live?
- What kind of work gives me energy?
- How much control over my schedule do I need?
- Which hobbies and routines are actually important?
- How much travel do I want?
- How much responsibility do I want to take for family?
- Do I want to build wealth only for myself or also for the next generation?
- Do I want to build something bigger, like a company, where the pure "enough" calculation becomes less relevant?

This should keep the article from becoming only a financial independence calculator.

## What I would change before reaching the number

One important question: if reaching "enough" would make me change my work, why not test some of that earlier?

Maybe I would try freelancing. Maybe I would work more on my own products. Maybe I would try entrepreneurship. But if these things matter, I should not wait until a perfect number is reached before learning whether I actually like them.

The risk is real. Maybe I am afraid of making the wrong decision. Maybe I tell myself I need full financial independence first because that feels cleaner and safer. But it might also delay useful experiments by years.

A thought I want to keep: even a dream job can feel like a normal job if I cannot decide my own schedule.

## Open questions

- How fast do I want to reach enough?
- Do I need to reach it, or is the pursuit already changing my choices?
- What would actually change the day after I reach it?
- Would the finish line move because of the hedonic treadmill?
- What safety net feels good enough for my personality?
- How does this change with family?
- How much money is for independence, and how much is for status?
- What would I keep doing even if I did not need the salary?

## Possible ending

There is probably no final answer. I will have to answer the question several times in life.

But I still think it is worth defining a current version of "enough", because otherwise I might chase a number without knowing what it is supposed to buy. The point is not to stop caring about money. The point is to know what role money should play, and where other things - attention, relationships, work, health, and flow - have to take over.

## Sources for the intro

- [Kahneman and Deaton (2010), *High income improves evaluation of life but not emotional well-being*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1011492107): the classic paper behind the $75,000 discussion. It separates emotional well-being from life evaluation.
- [Killingsworth (2021), *Experienced well-being rises with income, even above $75,000 per year*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2016976118): experience-sampling study that did not find the same plateau.
- [Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers (2023), *Income and emotional well-being: A conflict resolved*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2208661120): adversarial collaboration that reconciles the earlier disagreement in a more nuanced way.
- [Jebb, Tay, Diener, and Oishi (2018), *Happiness, income satiation and turning points around the world*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0277-0): global Gallup World Poll study with income-satiation estimates around $95,000 for life evaluation and $60,000 to $75,000 for emotional well-being.
- [Lindqvist, Ostling, and Cesarini (2020), *Long-run effects of lottery wealth on psychological well-being*](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdaa006): lottery-wealth study from Sweden. Wealth increased overall life satisfaction for many years, while effects on happiness and mental health were smaller.
- [Jantsch, Le Blanc, and Schmidt (2024), *Beyond income: Exploring the role of household wealth for subjective well-being in Germany*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-024-00811-1): useful because it looks at German wealth data and separates income, assets, debts, and relative wealth.
- [Donnelly et al. (2018), *The amount and source of millionaires' wealth (moderately) predict their happiness*](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167217744766): useful for the question whether self-earned and inherited wealth feel different.
- [Csikszentmihalyi (1999), *If we are so rich, why aren't we happy?*](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.10.821): directly connects wealth, happiness, and flow. Good source for the intro connection to *Flow*.
- [Mihaly and Isabella Csikszentmihalyi (1988), *Optimal Experience*, chapter 5](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511621956.005): useful source for the idea that flow depends on the fit between challenge, skill, attention, feedback, and the situation.
- [HarperAcademic page for *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience*](https://www.harperacademic.com/book/9780061339202/flow/): book page for the edition you are reading.
- [ZDF Terra Xplore with Leon Windscheid, *Wie viel Geld ist dir genug?*](https://www.zdf.de/video/reportagen/terra-xplore-die-psychologie-des-geldes-100/wie-viel-geld-ist-dir-genug-100): not the main citation source, but a useful German companion piece for social comparison and the psychology of money.
